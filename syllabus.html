1. Aims and learning outcomes

The module exists to make you a competent consumer and producer of analytics in a marketing setting. By the end you should be able to:

  1. Frame a marketing question as an analytics question: what is the unit of analysis, what would count as evidence, what decision does the answer change.
  2. Source data legally and sensibly, and defend the choice on coverage, granularity, reliability, cost and licence.
  3. Prepare data reproducibly: state the grain, join without exploding rows, treat missingness by mechanism, keep an audit trail.
  4. Describe and visualise without misleading: choose the chart from the question, show uncertainty, write captions that stand alone.
  5. Model with discipline: split first, establish a baseline, choose a metric that matches the cost structure, calibrate probabilities, check for leakage.
  6. Translate methods into marketing decisions: conjoint part-worths, choice probabilities, hedonic prices, response curves and budget allocation.
  7. Deliver honestly: a model card, a run log, a limitations section, and an explicit statement of what the analysis cannot support.

3. Session detail

Session 1 · Chapter 1 — Introduction

DIKW and the 5 V; data modalities and the pipeline; four levels of analytics, plus generative and agentic; the 2026 AI landscape from transformers to agents and retrieval; where analytics goes wrong: quality, bias, seven pitfalls. Take-away: a written analytics question with a named decision and a named metric.

Session 2 · Chapter 2 — Data sourcing

Levels of measurement and what each permits; classifying structured, semi-structured and unstructured data; scoring sources; APIs and REST; scraping and its legal limits; documents and LLM extraction; surveys, sampling, modes, nonresponse; experiments and A/B tests; governance and licences. Take-away: a source inventory with licence and quality notes.

Session 3 · Chapter 3 — Data preparation

Grain and keys; append, merge and the four join types; cardinality explosions; cleaning in five steps; MCAR, MAR and MNAR with simulated examples; imputation and what it costs; panels and balance; transformations; pipelines with pandera, dbt and drift monitoring. Take-away: an analysis table rebuilt from raw data by one command.

Session 4 · Chapter 4 — Description and visualization

Documentation and metadata from day one; centre, dispersion and shape with their robustness; correlation, contingency and conditional distributions; Simpson's paradox and restricted range; the grammar of graphics; dashboards, maps and Moran's I; the four families of misleading charts; accessibility. Take-away: a figure set with self-contained captions.

Session 5 · Chapter 5 — Machine learning

Four paradigms; prediction against causation; three splits and cross-validation; the leakage checklist; bias–variance; metrics, thresholds against business cost, calibration; linear and penalised models, k-NN, SVM, trees and ensembles; PCA and clustering; networks and reinforcement learning; model cards, MLOps, cost and latency. Take-away: a baseline plus an evaluation protocol you can defend.

Session 6 · Chapter 15 — Big data in marketing

Survey design and its error budget; conjoint types, attributes, levels and part-worths; random utility, logit, the IIA problem and the models that relax it; hedonic regression and quality-adjusted prices; response curves, elasticity and budget allocation; reading vendor claims critically. Take-away: a marketing analytics memo with one quantified recommendation.

4. Assessment and rubric

The module is assessed through the host course's project. Analytics enters 20% of the project rubric, under “Data analytics and visualization”, and shapes the evidence standard for the rest.

ComponentWeightWhat is judged
Participation and in-class work10%Preparation, discussion, exercises, responsible tool use
Project oral presentation30%Question, data, preliminary evidence, method, feasibility
Final essay and evidence package60%Integrated analysis, figures, reproducibility, reflection

The analytics rubric

CriterionWeight in the analytics componentWhat earns full marks
Question and decision15%The question is testable, the decision it informs is named, the metric follows from the cost of being wrong
Data sourcing and preparation25%Licence stated; grain stated; joins validated; missingness treated by mechanism; an audit trail exists
Analysis and interpretation25%Split before modelling; a baseline; the right metric; calibration where decisions depend on probability; no leakage
Code and reproducibility20%One command rebuilds everything; seeds fixed; outputs on disk; a run log per program
Communication15%Figures stand alone; captions carry the finding; limitations stated in the language of the decision

Submission requirements

  1. One PDF essay (≤ 4,000 words excluding appendix) plus an evidence package: code, data or a data-access statement, figures at full resolution, and run logs.
  2. A model card for any model you fit: purpose, data, metrics, limitations, ethical considerations.
  3. A leakage statement: which split you used, and why no information from the future or the target could enter the features.
  4. An AI-use appendix (see §6).
  5. Figures submitted as files, not pasted screenshots; every figure reproducible from the code you submit.

6. Policies

AI use

AI tools are allowed and expected — for code drafting, debugging, translation, and as a reading aid. They are not a substitute for verification. Every submission carries an appendix listing:

  • the tool and model used, and the date;
  • the purpose and the prompts or workflow;
  • the inputs you supplied and the outputs you used;
  • how you verified each output (ran it, checked a source, recomputed by hand);
  • what you corrected, and what you decided yourself.

Unverified AI output counts as academic misconduct, in the same category as a fabricated citation. A number you cannot reproduce is a number you cannot submit.

Data ethics and privacy

  • No personal data in any submission without consent and anonymisation; use aggregate or synthetic data where the question allows.
  • Respect terms of service and robots restrictions when collecting data; state the licence of every data set.
  • Do not scrape, store or redistribute material you have no right to use, even for a class exercise.
  • Report the limitations of your data as prominently as its findings.

Late work and resubmission

Late submissions lose 10% of the component per day, to a floor of 50%, unless an extension is agreed before the deadline. A failed analytics component may be resubmitted once within two weeks, with a written account of what changed.
