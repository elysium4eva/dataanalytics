What the chapter covers

Describing data so that the description survives scrutiny: documentation, the descriptive trio, correlation and conditional distributions, the grammar of graphics, dashboards, maps, and the ways charts lie.

Outline

§1Documentation and description
Four settings, documentation from day one, the 20-item checklist, metadata standards, an automatic data dictionary, data contracts, acceptance criteria, descriptive against inferential statistics
§2Univariate description
Central tendency, variability, distribution shape, skewness and kurtosis, robustness of the trio, five univariate charts, reading shape, univariate pitfalls
§3Bivariate and multivariate
Contingency tables, Pearson and Spearman correlation, covariance and slope, log transforms, how a restricted range weakens a correlation, small multiples, conditional distributions and Simpson's paradox
§4Chart grammar and choice
The grammar of graphics, which channel is read most accurately, choosing a chart by the question, conventions for figures and tables, axes and scales, colour, five-step chart choice
§5Tools, interactivity and geo-visualisation
The Python stack, Stata and R compared, interactive plotly charts, dashboards and their design principles, choropleth and symbol maps, classification methods, Moran's I and spatial autocorrelation, trade networks
§6Misleading, accessible, narrative
Truncated axes, area and 3D illusions, dual axes and cherry-picking, showing uncertainty, colour-blind friendly design, storytelling and AI-assisted charting
§7High-dimensional visualisation
Why high dimensions are hard, PCA, t-SNE and Isomap, reading a projection and its pitfalls, parallel coordinates, model-interpretation plots
§8Projects and wrap-up
Ten checks for an honest chart, project 1 charts, top ten chart mistakes, toolbox, glossary

Learning objectives

  • Write the documentation and metadata at the start of a project, not the end;
  • Describe a variable with centre, dispersion and shape, and say how robust each measure is;
  • Use correlation, contingency tables and conditional distributions without falling for Simpson's paradox or a restricted range;
  • Choose a chart from the grammar of graphics and write a caption that stands alone;
  • Build interactive charts, dashboards and maps, including a choropleth with a defensible classification;
  • Recognise and fix misleading charts, design for accessibility, and show uncertainty honestly.
